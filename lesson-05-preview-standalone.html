Difficult Conversations

Saying no, apologising properly, and giving (and receiving) feedback without damaging the relationship.

1 Warm-up

Everyone avoids the same conversations: saying no to a request, admitting a mistake, telling a colleague their work isn't good enough. The British way of handling them is to be clear about the message and generous about the person. Let's see where your instincts are.

1. Your manager asks you to take on another project, but you're overloaded. You say…

2. You sent a client the wrong figures. The best first line is…

3. A British colleague says your report has "a few small things to tweak". You should…

💡 A good "no" includes a reason and an alternative. A good apology owns the mistake before it explains it. Good feedback is specific, and British feedback is often more serious than it sounds.

3 The concept

Three difficult conversations

Three conversations cause most of the tension at work: saying no, apologising when you've got something wrong, and giving feedback someone may not want to hear. Browse the phrases for each, then try the quick check.

— Click either cell to see another example.

✅ Saying no: warm start + reason + alternative. "I'd love to, but I'm at capacity until the 15th — could I help with the next one instead?"
✅ Apologising: own it → fix it → prevent it. "That's my mistake. I've sent the right file. I'll double-check attachments from now on." Keep excuses out of the first sentence.
✅ Feedback: specific positive → one or two specific suggestions → a question. When receiving it: thank, ask for an example, don't defend yourself on the spot.
🎯 The tricky part for Polish speakers: British "sorry" and Polish przepraszam don't work the same way. British people say sorry constantly for tiny things ("Sorry, can I just squeeze past?"), so leaving it out can sound cold. But for real mistakes, the British expect a short apology that takes responsibility, not a long explanation of whose fault it was.

❌ "The system didn't save it, and nobody told me the deadline changed." → sounds like blaming.
✅ "I'm sorry — I missed the deadline change. I'll have it to you by 3."

When decoding British feedback, remember Lesson 2: "a few minor points" may be serious, and "have you considered…?" often means "you should."

Across cultures

UK

Say sorry for tiny things; for real mistakes, keep the apology short and own it. Feedback is often more serious than it sounds.

US

Criticism usually comes wrapped in praise (the "feedback sandwich"), so British understatement can be heard as approval.

Netherlands, Germany, East Asia

Dutch and German feedback tends to be direct and unsoftened. In much of East Asia, criticism in front of others causes serious loss of face: give it privately.

4 Reading — Three Sentences That Saved a Contract

When Kamil discovered that his team had sent a major client the wrong pricing, his first instinct was to explain. His draft email began with a long paragraph about a spreadsheet error, a colleague who had been off sick and a system update. His manager read it and deleted almost all of it. "They don't want the story," she said. "They want to know that you know, and that it's fixed."

The email they finally sent was three sentences long. It began “I'm sorry — we sent you the wrong pricing on Tuesday, and that's our mistake.” The second sentence gave the correct figures; the third explained what would stop it happening again. The client's reply was two words: “No worries.” What could have become a dispute was over in an afternoon, because the apology was accountable rather than defensive.

The same manager taught Kamil how to say no. When the sales director asked him to add a new feature two weeks before launch, Kamil's honest answer was "impossible": the team was already at capacity. Instead of saying so bluntly, he said “I'd love to, but we're flat out until launch — could we look at it for the June release?” The director agreed at once. A refusal with a reason and an alternative feels like a plan; a refusal without one feels like a closed door.

Feedback was the hardest skill. Kamil's reviews of junior colleagues' work were accurate but harsh, and people had stopped asking for them. He learnt to start with something specific that genuinely worked, then offer “One thing I'd suggest is…” and finish with a question. The content of his feedback barely changed; the way people received it changed completely. And when his own manager criticised him, he practised the hardest phrase of all: “Thanks, that's really useful — can you give me an example?” It is difficult to say when you want to justify yourself, which is exactly why it impresses people.
