Emails & Messaging

Openings and closings, chasing without nagging, apologising for delays, and the unwritten rules of Slack and Teams.

1 Warm-up

Written messages lose your smile, your tone of voice and your chance to explain. Every word has to do that work instead. A British reader forms an opinion of you from the first line and the sign-off, often before reading the middle.

1. You're emailing Helen Clarke, a client you've never met. The best opening is…

2. A colleague hasn't replied for a week. Your follow-up starts…

3. On Slack or Teams, you need a quick answer from someone. You send…

💡 Match the formality of the person you're writing to, and move one step less formal once they do. Chase with "just following up", never "I'm still waiting". In chat, put the greeting and the question in the same message.

3 The concept

The three jobs of a work message

Most work messages do one of three jobs: they open and close at the right level of formality, they ask or chase without sounding pushy, or they apologise and clarify when something has gone wrong. Browse each, then try the quick check.

✅ The formality ladder: Dear Ms Clarke → Dear Helen → Hi Helen → Helen → (no greeting, in a thread). Start one step more formal than you think you need, then copy the other person.
✅ Structure: greeting → one-line opener → the point (in the first two lines) → the request + deadline → a warm close. Busy people read the top and skim the rest.
✅ Chat (Slack/Teams): greeting + question in one message; use threads; don't message someone's private chat for something a channel should see; respect status ("Away", "Focusing").
🎯 The tricky part for Polish speakers: some very natural Polish email formulas sound strange or pushy when translated.

❌ "Respected Mr Kowalski" (Szanowny Panie) → ✅ "Dear Mr Kowalski"
❌ "I am waiting for your answer" (Czekam na odpowiedź) → ✅ "I look forward to hearing from you"
❌ "Greetings" (Pozdrawiam) as a sign-off → ✅ "Best wishes" / "Kind regards"
❌ "ASAP" to a client → ✅ "as soon as you can" or, better, a real date.

And avoid "Dear Sir or Madam" if you know (or can find) the person's name.

Across cultures

UK

"Hi Helen" / "Best wishes" is the everyday default. "Cheers" is common between colleagues. Replying within one working day is normal courtesy.

US

Shorter, more upbeat emails: "Hi Helen," and "Best,". "Cheers" is rarely used.

Central Europe, Asia

"Dear Mr/Ms + surname" lasts much longer in German and many Central European companies. Many Asian cultures expect a longer relationship-building opening before the request.

4 Reading — The Email That Waited

Agnieszka, a project manager in a Birmingham engineering firm, had a supplier who never answered her emails on time. After two weeks of silence she wrote, "I am still waiting for your answer. Please reply ASAP." The reply came within an hour, and it was noticeably cold. What she had meant as persistence had come across as a reprimand.

Her British colleague Dan showed her his own version of a chaser: “Just following up on my email below — any news on the delivery date?” It says the same thing, but it quietly assumes that the other person is busy, not lazy. For a second reminder he might write “Just bumping this to the top of your inbox”, a slightly playful line that makes a nudge feel friendly rather than passive-aggressive.

Dan's other rule was about tone at the edges of an email. People skim the middle, he said, but they notice the greeting and the sign-off. He opened new client emails with "Dear" and closed them with “Kind regards”, then relaxed to "Hi" and "Best wishes" once the client did. When he was late replying, he didn't write a paragraph of excuses. He wrote “Apologies for the slow reply” and answered the question in the next line.

Chat tools have their own etiquette. On the team's Slack, Agnieszka used to send "Hi" and wait for a response before asking her question, which left colleagues feeling put on the spot. Now she writes “Hi Mark — quick one: is the deck final?” in a single message. And when a 40-person email arrived asking for comments, she resisted the urge to reply all. Her one-line "Thanks, looks good" went only to the sender, which saved 39 people a notification and made her look quietly professional.
