Meetings & Calls

Getting a word in, disagreeing without damage, keeping things on track — in the room and on video.

1 Warm-up

In British meetings, what you say matters, but so does how you get the floor and how you disagree. People who are silent can seem uninterested, and people who interrupt bluntly can seem aggressive. The space in between is a set of fairly simple phrases.

1. You want to add a point while a colleague is still speaking. You say…

2. At the end of a meeting, someone says "Let's take that away and think about it." This often means…

3. On a video call, the host has been talking for 10 seconds without sound. You say…

💡 Apologise briefly to get the floor ("Sorry to jump in"), acknowledge before disagreeing ("I take your point, but…"), and use "we" to steer ("Shall we come back to that?"). Silence in a British meeting is rarely read as agreement; it's more often read as having nothing to say.
2 Quick check — before we explain anything

Four quick questions. This isn't graded harshly — it just shows us where to focus.

1. You disagree with your manager's plan in a meeting. The best way in is…

2. The discussion has drifted off topic. As chair, you say…

3. Someone interrupts you before you've finished. You say…

4. A quiet colleague hasn't said anything. To include her, you say…

3 The concept

Three meeting skills

Three skills make you effective in a meeting: getting the floor (and keeping it), disagreeing diplomatically, and keeping things on track, which isn't only the chair's job. Browse each, then try the quick check.

— Click either cell to see another example.

✅ Get the floor: short apology + "just" + your point. "Sorry to jump in — just a quick one on the budget." Then keep it short.
✅ Disagree: acknowledge → concern → suggestion. "I take your point. I'm a bit worried about the timeline. Could we look at a phased launch?"
✅ Keep on track: park it, be conscious of time, recap, and name an owner. Use "we" and "shall we" so steering sounds shared.
🎯 The tricky part for Polish speakers: two opposite risks.

Too direct: "No, it's not true" / "You are wrong" (To nieprawda) sounds hostile in a British meeting. → "I'm not sure that's quite the case."
Too quiet: waiting to be asked, or waiting until you've formed a perfect sentence. British meetings reward people who come in early with a short point. "Can I just add something?" is enough to start.

Also decode the British "maybe": "Let's take that away", "We'll bear that in mind" and "That's worth thinking about" often mean no decision, or no.
🌍 Elsewhere: in American meetings people often interrupt more freely and speak more confidently about half-formed ideas. In Japan, Finland and parts of Asia, silence and pauses are comfortable and interrupting is rare. Dutch and Israeli colleagues may disagree very directly and see it as honesty. On video: camera on for external calls unless told otherwise; mute when not speaking; say "You're on mute" early and kindly; use the chat or a raised hand to get the floor in big calls.

4 Reading — Why Nobody Heard Ola

Ola was the best analyst on her team in Leeds, and almost nobody in senior management knew it. In the weekly meeting she waited politely for a gap in the conversation, but in a room full of fluent speakers the gap never came. By the time she had put her point into a perfect sentence, the discussion had moved on. Her manager assumed she had nothing to add, and her silence was read as a lack of initiative.

A mentor gave her two phrases. The first was “Sorry to jump in — can I just add something?” It felt rude at first, but nobody in the room even noticed. In British meetings a short apology is the normal price of entry. The second was “Building on what Sam said…”, which let her join a discussion by connecting to someone else's idea, so her contribution sounded collaborative rather than competitive.

Disagreeing was harder. Ola's instinct was either to stay silent or to say what she really thought: "That won't work." Her mentor suggested a three-step pattern: acknowledge, raise a concern, offer an alternative. “I take your point, but I'm a bit worried about the timeline” turned a confrontation into a problem the whole group could solve. Within a month, two of her suggestions had been adopted.

She also learnt to read the room. When the director said “Let's take that away and think about it”, it usually meant the idea would quietly stall. And when discussions began to drift, the person who said “Shall we park that and come back to it at the end?” was usually the one people saw as a future leader. A year later, Ola was chairing the meeting herself. Her final recap at the end of each one, “So, just to recap…”, became the part everyone waited for.
