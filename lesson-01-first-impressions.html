Warm-up

Most first impressions at work are made in under two minutes, and almost none of that time is spent on anything important. That's the point: small talk is how British colleagues decide whether you're easy to work with. Let's see what your instincts say.

Key finding
In British workplaces, "How are you?" is a greeting, not a question. First names are normal almost immediately, even with senior people, once they've offered theirs. The rule of thumb: follow the other person's lead.

The three moves of any first conversation

The concept

Every short professional conversation has three moves: you open it (greet, introduce, get names right), you keep it going (safe topics, follow-up questions, small reactions), and you close it gracefully. Browse the phrases for each move, then try the quick check.

Click either cell to see another example.

Open: name + one detail. "I'm Kasia — I look after the Kraków office." Then use their name once, early, so it sticks.
Keep it going: safe topic → open question → follow-up. Journey, the event, the weekend, holidays, work in general. Not salary, politics, religion, age or anyone's weight.
Close: a softener + a reason or a next step + a warm line. "Anyway, I'll let you get back to it — lovely to meet you."
The tricky part for Polish speakers
in Polish, Pan Tomasz or Pani Kasia is perfectly polite. In English, a title goes only with a surname, never a first name.

Avoid"Mr Tom", "Mrs Helen" — sounds like a child talking to a teacher, or a hotel receptionist.
Use"Tom" (once he's introduced himself as Tom) or "Mr Clarke" (only in very formal first contact, e.g. an email to someone you've never met).

Also: in British English there is no full stop after Mr, Mrs, Ms or Dr. Use Ms for a woman unless you know she prefers something else.

04

The First Ninety Seconds

Reading

When Marta moved from Warsaw to a consultancy in London, her English was better than that of half her new colleagues. Her first week still felt oddly difficult. At her welcome drinks, the managing partner came over, smiled and said, “Hi, I'm James — I don't think we've met properly.” Marta, anxious to be respectful, replied, "Good evening, Mr James." He laughed kindly, but she saw the awkward flicker in his eyes and spent the rest of the evening replaying it.

Her mistake was not grammar. It was register: the level of formality a situation expects. British offices tend to hide hierarchy rather than display it. Senior people introduce themselves by first name, and the expected response is simply “Nice to meet you, James.” Being too formal doesn't come across as respect. It creates distance, and in a culture that prizes being approachable, distance can be mistaken for coldness.

Small talk, which many learners dismiss as trivial, is where most of this happens. A question like “Did you have far to come?” is not really about the journey. It's a low-risk way of checking that the other person is friendly and easy to talk to. The best conversationalists are rarely the most interesting people in the room; they are the ones who ask “Oh really? How did you end up doing that?” and then actually listen to the answer.

Leaving a conversation matters too. Marta's instinct was to wait until the other person walked away, which meant she often stood stranded with one person for an hour. A colleague taught her an exit line: “Anyway, I'll let you get back to it — lovely to meet you.” It sounds considerate, it's courteous, and nobody has ever been offended by it. By her third event, Marta was using “Sorry, I didn't quite catch your name” without embarrassment, and a rapport with her new team followed soon after. As she put it later, "Nobody taught me any of this at school, because none of it is written down."
